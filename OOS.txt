Introduction
Context: A critical logistics use case: handling Out-of-Stock (OOS) situations in the U.S. market. In this “push” distribution model (brewery → wholesaler without advance wholesaler orders),ABI faces frequent OOS alerts raised by wholesalers when inventory of certain SKUs runs low[2]. 

Current Process & Challenges

Current OOS Handling Process: When a wholesaler anticipates or experiences a stockout of a beer product (SKU), they notify ABI’s central Deployment/Control Tower team via two channels[2]: 
•	Channel 1: Ticket Portal – The wholesaler submits an OOS request through a portal, which creates a JIRA ticket (moving to ServiceNow) that enters a queue for coordinators[2]. This is used for lower-risk situations (e.g. still ~5 days of inventory on hand). 
•	Channel 2: Hotline Call – For urgent, high-risk cases (imminent or current stockout), the wholesaler calls a hotline. A coordinator takes the call, manually logs a JIRA ticket on the wholesaler’s behalf, and then begins resolution[2]. 

Manual Resolution Workflow: Once notified via either channel, a logistics coordinator must: 
1.	Review the request: Read the free-form description (e.g. “Need 10 more pallets of ASAP”) and interpret the SKU and quantity needed[1]. 
2.	Validate inventory status: Check the Product Supply system for that wholesaler’s current inventory, days-of-cover remaining, and any incoming shipments already scheduled[1]. If the data shows no risk (e.g. a delivery is arriving tomorrow), the coordinator may decide no action is needed and inform the wholesaler[1]. Otherwise, the coordinator proceeds to plan a remedy. 
3.	Determine a solution: Evaluate options to fulfill the shortage – e.g. add the needed quantity to an upcoming shipment, pull forward a later shipment, or source from an alternate brewery or warehouse – all while minimizing cost and meeting the delivery need[1]. This often involves checking multiple systems and considering constraints (truck capacities, production availability, transit times). 
4.	Execute and communicate: Update the order/shipment in the system (e.g. update the Supply chain management system, SSM) to allocate the extra stock and schedule delivery[1]. Finally, close the loop by informing the wholesaler of the resolution (or if none is possible) and closing the ticket. 


Proposed Solution (AI-Driven OOS Copilot)

The team proposed a Generative AI Copilot system that would streamline OOS handling from request to resolution, reducing human workload and response time. The envisioned solution works as follows:
1.	Unified Digital Intake: All wholesalers would use a single interface (e.g. the existing portal or a chatbot) to report OOS scenarios, ensuring structured data capture. If a phone call comes in, the idea is to funnel it into the same digital workflow (for example, a support agent or speech-to-text could create the ticket behind the scenes). This eliminates parallel processes – all requests become trackable tickets for the AI to process[2]. The request form could be simplified (perhaps just selecting the SKU and quantity needed) to reduce free-text ambiguity. 
2.	Immediate AI Triage: As soon as an OOS ticket is created, the GenAI Copilot will ingest its details. Leveraging NLP, the AI can parse any text to identify the product SKU, the quantity shortfall, and timing urgency[1]. (For example, “Need 10 cases of BrandY by this Friday” would be converted into a structured ask for 10 cases of BrandY). This parsing replaces the human reading step. The AI could even prompt for missing info if needed (e.g. ask the wholesaler via chat for clarification). 
3.	Automated Inventory Check (Validation): Next, the Copilot will cross-check current inventory and supply data for that wholesaler. It will query ABI’s systems (through secure connectors or APIs) to retrieve the wholesaler’s on-hand inventory, open orders, and upcoming deliveries for the SKU in question[1]. With this data, the AI determines if the OOS alert truly requires action. For instance, if data shows a shipment arriving later that day covering the shortfall, the AI could decide no intervention is needed and inform the wholesaler of the incoming delivery. If the risk is confirmed (e.g. stock will run out before the next delivery or there is no delivery scheduled), the Copilot proceeds to resolution planning. This automated validation mirrors what the coordinator does manually, but in seconds. Notably, ABI confirmed that the relevant data (inventory levels, forecasts, etc.) is available in structured format and can be made accessible to the AI system[1], enabling this step.
4.	Decision/Planning (AI Resolution Finder): For confirmed stockouts, the Copilot will generate a resolution plan. Using business rules and optimization logic, it considers the same options a human would – e.g.: 
a.	Is there an upcoming shipment to that wholesaler (or nearby location) that can be augmented with the needed quantity? 
b.	If not, is there excess inventory at a nearby distribution center or brewery that could be dispatched as a special delivery? 
c.	What is the least-cost way to get additional stock there in time (perhaps choosing between air freight vs. deferred, or sourcing from different locations)? 
The AI essentially coordinates across various data points (production availability, transit times, costs) to propose the best solution[1]. This might be achieved via a combination of heuristic rules and AI evaluation: for example, the AI could call a function to simulate different supply options and then use a GPT-based reasoning to pick the optimal one given the constraints. The result of this step would be a concrete plan: “Add X units of SKU to truck #123 arriving Thursday” or “Dispatch emergency shipment from Brewery B arriving in 2 days.” The workshop noted that currently a human does this mentally; with AI, an algorithmic approach can ensure all options are considered systematically[1]. (For instance, ABI mentioned developing a “validation model” and an algorithm so that the AI can incorporate complex business rules like least-cost-path shipping[1].) Throughout, the AI would respect ABI’s operational constraints and targets (not making promises it can’t fulfill).
5.	Execution & Ticket Update: Once a resolution is decided, the Copilot will take action in the appropriate systems. This could mean programmatically updating the order or shipment in the supply chain system (SSM) – for example, adding the SKU and quantity to a delivery order – or creating a new transfer order if needed[1]. Essentially, the AI agent would perform the same data entry a coordinator would, via integrations or function calls. After execution, it would update the ServiceNow/JIRA ticket with the solution details and mark it as resolved. Importantly, the Copilot would continue to monitor the ticket until delivery is confirmed[2]. One key improvement is that the AI won’t prematurely close the ticket; it can wait and verify (via data feeds) that the stock actually got delivered to the wholesaler before finally closing, thus addressing the current 5–10% premature closure issue[2].
6.	Wholesaler Communication: In parallel with executing the fix, the system can automatically notify the wholesaler. For example, the Copilot could send a chat or email: “Your request has been processed – an extra 10 cases will arrive on Truck #123 scheduled for delivery tomorrow by 5 PM.” This instant feedback greatly improves wholesaler visibility compared to waiting hours for an update. If the AI determined no action was needed (e.g. “your upcoming shipment already covers this”), it would communicate that reasoning as well. Thus, the wholesaler is kept informed in near-real-time, improving trust in the system.
7.	Exceptional Cases: If the AI cannot confidently find a solution (e.g. a very unusual scenario or conflicting data), it will escalate to a human coordinator with its analysis. The coordinator can then intervene for that case. Over time, as the AI learns from more scenarios, the need for escalation should diminish. Initially, a human-in-the-loop approach may be used – e.g., the AI drafts a resolution and a human approves it – to build confidence in the Copilot’s decisions[1]. 
Solution Highlights: This AI Copilot essentially acts as an autonomous logistics coordinator that can read, reason, and react. Key aspects of the proposed solution include: 
•	Speed: Immediate processing of requests 24/7, potentially cutting response time from ~8.5 hours down to minutes[1]. Even if final execution (physical delivery) still takes time, the wholesaler gets an instant plan of action. 
•	Consistency: Every ticket is handled with the same rules and thorough data analysis, reducing variability. 
•	Integrated Automation: By directly interfacing with inventory databases and order systems, the AI eliminates dozens of manual look-ups and clicks per ticket. 
•	Natural Language Understanding: The Copilot can handle free-form input gracefully, parsing out what’s needed from the wholesaler’s message[1]. This makes the system user-friendly while structured under the hood.
•	Closing the Loop: Automated monitoring means a ticket isn’t considered done until the product is delivered, ensuring follow-through (the AI can keep the ticket “open” and even generate reminders or follow-ups if a planned shipment gets delayed, etc.). 
During the workshop, the team emphasized that much of the required data (inventory levels, shipments, production plans) is available and accessible for AI via APIs, which is a big enabler[1]. They also discussed process tweaks like ensuring each SKU shortage is logged separately so the AI can address them one by one[1]. The desired end state is a single seamless workflow where a wholesaler’s request is instantly understood and resolved by an AI agent, drastically reducing stockout durations and manual labor.
